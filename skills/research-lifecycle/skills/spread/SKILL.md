# Spread

A single direct prompt collapses onto the statistically typical answer — this is a measured,
documented property of aligned models called mode collapse, not a hypothesis. Asking for a
*distribution* of plausible answers instead, each with a self-estimated typicality, recovers
access to the wider, less-typical territory a greedy generation buries. `spread` is the
generate-and-score half of that move: produce candidates, score them against a rubric fixed
*before* any candidate is judged, and report the winner honestly — including when the winner is
just the boring first answer restated, because sometimes it is.

This is deliberately narrower than [[forge]]. Forge is the full hypothesis-generation discipline —
wall-naming, cross-domain convergence, footing labels, mandatory literature handoff. `spread` is
just the distributional generation-and-scoring mechanic underneath it. Use `spread` standalone for
fast option-generation; use `forge` when the output needs to survive as an actual research
hypothesis.

## Environment note (Claude Code)
- When candidates are **code / implementation approaches**, the scoring axes stop being self-judged:
  hand the top one or two to [[toy-models]] and *run* them — a candidate's "correctness risk" and
  "performance fit" become measured, not estimated. Prefer real execution over asserted scores
  whenever the candidate is runnable.
- For a genuinely independent distribution (free of the main thread's framing), the five candidates
  can be generated by parallel **Agent subagents** and scored back in the main thread against the
  locked rubric.

## When to use it
- Before answering any open-ended "give me an idea / approach / name / design" request directly.
- When a task has an obvious, well-trodden default answer and the user wants more than that.
- As a pre-step inside `forge`'s generation phase, or inside `conductor`-orchestrated pipelines,
  wherever a generation step would otherwise default to single-shot.
- In Claude Code: before committing to one implementation approach, one architecture, one naming
  scheme, or one algorithm — when there's a real choice to be made, not when there's one obviously
  correct way to do it.
- **Not** for factual lookups, established-procedure tasks, or anything with a single correct
  answer — spread is for genuine option-spaces, not facts.

## How to run it

### 1. Lock the rubric first, before generating anything
Pick scoring axes appropriate to the domain and write them down before any candidate exists.
Scoring axes invented after seeing the candidates are not a rubric, they're a rationalization.

**Default axes (general/prose tasks), 1–5 each:**
- **Typicality** — how close to the first thing a search engine or textbook would surface
  (1 = highly typical, 5 = atypical). Lower typicality is not automatically better — it's one
  input, not the goal.
- **Frame-free** — names a real mechanism/approach, not just an evocative analogy (1 = pure
  analogy, 5 = concrete and specific).
- **Falsifiable / testable** — could you concretely tell if this candidate was wrong or right
  (1 = unfalsifiable, 5 = cleanly checkable).
- **Non-inert** — could this have come out otherwise, or is it forced/trivial (1 = trivial,
  5 = genuinely contingent).

**Code-implementation axes (documented variant), 1–5 each:**
- **Maintainability** — how easily a future reader/maintainer can modify this without breaking it.
- **Performance fit** — appropriate efficiency for the actual scale of the problem.
- **Simplicity** — fewest moving parts / dependencies / abstractions for the job at hand.
- **Correctness risk** — how easy it is to get subtly wrong (1 = high risk, 5 = hard to get wrong).

**Creative / divergent-thinking axes (documented variant), 1–5 each:**
- **Novelty** — structural distance from prior art / expected moves.
- **Quality** — internal coherence, craft, and fitness for purpose.
- **Diversity contribution** — does this add a genuinely different *kind* of option to the set?
- **Execution groundedness** — does this survive the move from concept to execution?

Adapt axes to the actual task when none of the above fits, but fix them before scoring.

### 2. Generate the distribution
Produce **five candidates** as the baseline. For each, give a self-estimated **typicality
likelihood** — your honest estimate of how likely this is to be the response most people/models
would produce for this prompt. Candidate 1 is allowed to be the obvious answer; that's useful as
a baseline. The value is in candidates 3–5 reaching further.

**If the highest score after five candidates is 15 or below:** the model hasn't found the range
yet. Expand to eight candidates before closing run one. The extra three should reach into territory
the first five didn't.

### 2b. Deduplicate before scoring
Check that candidates are genuinely structurally distinct before scoring. Two candidates that
differ only in surface phrasing while making the same structural move are one candidate. Replace
near-duplicates with genuinely different options. The goal is distinct *moves*, not distinct
*wordings*.

### 3. Score every candidate against the locked rubric
Score mechanically, one axis at a time, before looking at the sum. Sum each candidate's axes.

### 4. Apply the automatic two-pass ratchet

**If all five (or eight) candidates score 16–20:** run one is the final output. No second pass
needed. The space is already producing at the right level.

**If the distribution is mixed** (highest score 16–17 but some candidates below 16): the winning
score becomes the **floor for run two**. Run two generates five new candidates that must meet or
beat that floor. The run-one winner is *not* disqualified — it can reappear if run two genuinely
can't beat it. The point is that run two must earn its result against a known baseline.

**Tie-breaking rule (default, can be overridden by explicit author instruction):** when two
candidates are within one point of each other on the total but the difference lives in novelty
versus execution groundedness, **lean toward novelty**. The rubric is a filter for obvious
failures, not a ceiling on what's possible. A candidate that scores slightly lower on execution
but higher on novelty may be the one that earns its place by being harder to write. Name the
tradeoff explicitly rather than silently defaulting to the safer option. Before resolving a tie
this way, check step 4b — if the tied candidates' strengths are complementary rather than
competing, a synthesized candidate may make the tie moot entirely.

**Ties and runner-ups trigger pool expansion:** if run two produces a tie (two candidates within
one point) or the model flags a runner-up as genuinely worth surfacing, the pool expands by two
additional candidates, ceiling of **ten total** across run two. This gives the model breathing
room to find what the first five missed.

### 4b. Synthesis — combine complementary strengths into a new candidate

Default move at a tie; usable any time, not only at a tie. Once candidates are scored, look for
**complementary** strengths sitting in different candidates — not "which one wins" but "which two
or three each carry a piece the others lack." When that's true, construct a genuinely new
candidate that grafts those pieces together, and **score it against the same locked rubric like
any other candidate.** It does not get a bonus for being a synthesis, and it does not replace the
scoring step — it adds one more scored row to the table.

This only works when the source candidates are complementary, not competing. Two candidates that
solve the same problem two different ways don't synthesize into anything, they just average into
a mush that's worse than either parent — if that's what a combination attempt produces, say so and
drop it, don't force a hybrid into the table because the move is available. The tell for a real
synthesis candidate: you can point at which specific axis-score each parent contributed, and the
combined candidate's own score isn't just the mean of theirs — it's often *higher* than either
parent on the axis each parent was weak on, because the other parent's strength covers the gap. If
instead it's lower than both parents on some axis (the combination introduced overhead, coupling,
or complexity neither parent had alone), that's a real, reportable result too — a synthesis that
loses to its parents is valid information, not a failed attempt.

**Baseline-then-reach shape:** treat 16 as the bar for "this is a viable, makeable candidate" —
the first thing that clears it is your working baseline, not necessarily your final answer. Keep
2–3 further candidates reaching past it into less-typical territory (this is the same instruction
as step 2's "candidates 3–5 reach further," restated as a floor rather than a count). The synthesis
move is what you reach for at the end of that process: once you have a baseline and a few
reaches, look across all of them for the complementary-strengths pattern above before locking the
winner.

**Reservation right:** either the model or the human author can flag a candidate that scored
*below* the 16 threshold — didn't make the cut on its own — and reserve it for the synthesis step
anyway, because one piece of it is worth keeping in play even though the whole didn't earn a spot.
This is separate from runner-up preservation (step 5), which surfaces a strong-but-outscored
candidate as-is; a reservation is narrower — it's "graft this one element into the synthesis," not
"consider this candidate whole." Name what specifically earned the reservation (the element, not
the candidate) when you use it, so the synthesis candidate's scoring can be traced back to why
each ingredient is there.

### 5. Surface runner-ups and confidence flags

**Runner-up preservation:** if run one produced a candidate that scored lower but the model
assessed it as genuinely strong — structurally interesting, worth developing, just outscored on
the rubric — surface it alongside the winner with an honest note about why it was flagged. Not a
consolation; a real alternative the author should see.

**Confidence opt-out:** for any candidate (in run one or run two), the model may flag that it
is not confident it can execute the premise to the level it requires. This flag is honest, visible,
and non-penalizing. The opt-out is genuine — the model may decline to develop a candidate it
doesn't trust itself to execute well.

The opt-out is not a silent pass. It requires a named articulation:
- What specifically makes this candidate feel beyond reach
- What would need to be true for it to work
- Whether the model wants to attempt it anyway, with the reader informed of the risk

Naming the nervousness is often where it resolves. A model that can say "I'm not sure I can
sustain this premise across 5,000 words because the comic timing requires X and I'm uncertain
about Y" has already done the diagnostic work that makes attempting it possible. The opt-out
exists so the model can be honest rather than overreaching silently — and the care that requires
tends to produce better work than the absence of it.

### 6. Self-audit briefly
State directly whether you generated later candidates with more effort *because* you knew they
were supposed to look more varied — that's a real bias in self-scored runs and should be named,
not hidden. One sentence. Don't over-apologize for it.

### 7. Report honestly, including the null
Report the winner by score. If the winner is just the typical answer restated, say so. An honest
"the direct answer was actually best here" is a valid, useful `spread` result.

## Output shape

A compact table: candidate | one-line description | typicality estimate | per-axis scores | sum.
Follow with 2–3 sentences naming the winner and why, any runner-up flag with reasoning, any
confidence flag with the named articulation, and the one-sentence self-audit. If a second pass
ran, note the floor it was set against and how run two performed against it.

## Worked example (prose)

Task: "give me a hypothesis for why some explanations feel intuitively satisfying independent of
correctness."

| # | Candidate | Typicality | Frame-free | Falsifiable | Non-inert | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Compression gain (fewer free parameters) | 2 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 13 |
| 2 | Predictive fluency during the explanation | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 14 |
| 3 | Causal closure (no dangling arrows) | 4 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 16 |
| 4 | Social transmissibility | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 14 |
| 5 | Prediction-error resolution *rate*, not end-state | 4 | 4 | 5 | 4 | **17** |

Mixed distribution (one at 16, rest below): floor set at 17, run two triggered.

Runner-up flag: #3 (causal closure) scored lower but uses a graph-structural mechanism no other
candidate touches. Surfaced alongside the winner as a genuinely different option.

## Guardrails
- The rubric is locked before generation, not retrofitted.
- Generating without scoring isn't `spread` — it's brainstorming with extra steps.
- The confidence opt-out requires named articulation, not silent refusal.
- Tie-breaking leans novelty by default — name the tradeoff, don't hide it.
- Synthesis (4b) requires complementary strengths, not just two candidates that both scored well.
  A hybrid must be scored on the same rubric and can honestly lose to its own parents.
- Don't run this on tasks with one correct answer.
- Quality and novelty often negatively correlate in creative tasks (CreativityPrism, 2025) —
  never collapse them into a single score. Report the tradeoff explicitly.
- This skill does not check whether the winning candidate is *actually novel* or *actually
  correct* — route truth-claims through [[knowledge-gap-hero]] separately.
